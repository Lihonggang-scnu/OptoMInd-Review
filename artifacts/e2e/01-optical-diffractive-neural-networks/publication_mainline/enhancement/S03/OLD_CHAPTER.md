The physical realization of trained diffractive optical neural networks spans a diverse landscape of hardware platforms, ranging from static printed phase plates and lithographically fabricated multilevel structures to dynamically reconfigurable spatial light modulators and metasurfaces [[claim:S03-P057]]. This diversity reflects a fundamental tension in optical computing: diffractive deep neural networks offer ultra-low latency and passive energy efficiency by performing computation through light diffraction alone, yet their hardware implementation faces significant challenges in scalability and dynamic reconfigurability relative to electronic systems [[claim:S03-P129]]. However, the choice of platform ultimately determines which of these theoretical benefits can be realized in practice.

All-dielectric metasurfaces have emerged as a dominant physical platform for realizing static diffractive deep neural networks, leveraging subwavelength structural elements to encode learned phase profiles directly into fabricated nanostructures [[claim:S03-P034]]. By operating at scales below the diffraction limit, metasurface-based implementations overcome the large pixel size limitations inherent to conventional computer-generated holography, enabling compact, high-resolution all-optical machine learning systems [[claim:S03-P033]]. These structures process information through coherent light field modulation, providing an alternative to architecture-limited conventional computing platforms with inherent massive parallelism and energy efficiency. The fully passive nature of such all-dielectric metasurface implementations means they offer low-latency computation without requiring external power for phase modulation during inference [[claim:S03-P121]]. However, conventional metasurfaces function as static, write-once devices whose optical properties are permanently fixed during fabrication, inherently restricting their adaptability for applications requiring dynamic reconfiguration or task switching [[claim:S03-P073]].

Fabrication techniques for these phase-modulating structures span multiple paradigms, each imposing distinct tolerances on the final optical performance. Diffractive deep neural networks have been physically realized using additive manufacturing techniques, where 3D-printed phase structures demonstrate experimental performance that aligns closely with numerical simulations, validating rapid prototyping as a viable pathway for static ODNN hardware [[claim:S03-P041]]. For applications prioritizing manufacturability over maximum accuracy, minimalist optical diffractive networks employing two-level quantized pixel-wise phase modulation offer a fabrication-friendly alternative to continuous-phase devices, trading some classification accuracy for significantly reduced manufacturing complexity and cost [[claim:S03-P085]]. Beyond lithographic and additive approaches, solution-based chemical methods such as sol-gel processes provide scalable and low-cost alternatives for fabricating large-area porous films with tunable refractive indices, contrasting favorably with more expensive lithographic techniques for diffractive element production [[claim:S03-P007]]. The geometric details of coating processes also matter substantially: coating geometry and directional deposition angles significantly influence film thickness uniformity and interfacial stress in thin films, which directly impacts the phase profile fidelity achievable in practical diffractive optical elements [[claim:S03-P008]].

Beyond purely static implementations, several reconfigurable approaches attempt to bridge the gap between fixed-function hardware and adaptive optical computing. Multi-wavelength diffractive deep neural networks enable multi-task learning by encoding different tasks into distinct optical wavelengths, thereby avoiding the need for mechanical reconfiguration or separate hardware instances for each task [[claim:S03-P025]]. This wavelength-multiplexed approach allows parallel processing of multiple inputs across spectral channels, significantly improving computing throughput without additional physical footprint. Hardware-in-the-loop methodologies complement such approaches by enabling end-to-end design and optimization of diffractive optical elements, incorporating physical measurement feedback from programmable phase spatial light modulators directly into the training loop [[claim:S03-P052]]. Meanwhile, integrated rewritable metasurfaces promise on-chip reconfigurable diffractive neural networks but currently exhibit a notable simulation-to-reality gap associated with fabrication imperfections and device non-idealities that affect trained performance when deployed [[claim:S03-P049]].

Bridging this simulation-to-deployment gap requires physics-aware differentiable codesign frameworks that account for the discrete, analog nature of real optical devices during training. Directly adding a discrete mapping from physical device responses to diffractive optical neural network parameters breaks the gradient chain in backpropagation, necessitating novel codesign approaches that preserve differentiability through mechanisms such as Gumbel-Softmax relaxation [[claim:S03-P020]]. Furthermore, physics-aware roughness optimization techniques are employed during fabrication to address performance sensitivity to manufacturing imperfections [[claim:S03-P062]]. These approaches recognize that trainable parameters in diffractive systems are constrained not only by discrete device mappings with irregular analog responses but also by fundamental physics—for instance, phase modulation is inherently periodic with a 2π period that must be respected during optimization.

Environmental stability and mechanical robustness represent persistent challenges for multi-layer diffractive systems. Photonic neural network resonators, when used for implementing activation functions, exhibit nonlinear behavior advantageous for computation but remain vulnerable to environmental factors such as temperature fluctuations and mechanical vibrations that can adversely impact performance [[claim:S03-P002]]. The operational stability of diffractive optical neural networks is further constrained by spatial coherence requirements: practical deployment assumes quasimonochromatic illumination conditions with coherence lengths exceeding the maximum path length difference between diffractive layers, assumptions that may not hold for many real-world incoherent light applications [[claim:S03-P022]]. Multi-layer diffractive optical networks offer improved generalization and application-specific design performance but simultaneously introduce significant practical challenges related to fabrication complexity and opto-mechanical alignment sensitivity [[claim:S03-P107]]. Misalignment resilience has therefore become a critical hardware stability factor, with research demonstrating that introducing random layer-to-layer displacements during training can substantially improve error tolerance margins against physical misalignments [[claim:S03-P108]].

Looking forward, emerging integration strategies aim to consolidate functionality while navigating fundamental physical constraints. Diffractive neural network implementations remain constrained by the Abbe-Rayleigh diffraction limit, which restricts the minimum feature size and spatial resolution of phase modulation elements in traditional optical setups [[claim:S03-P089]]. Cascade diffractive architectures address some of these limitations by circumventing the need for explicit nonlinear electronic activation functions, relying instead on optical modulation and free-space propagation to achieve computational depth [[claim:S03-P097]]. Nevertheless, most on-chip diffractive neural network architectures remain fixed-function devices that lack dynamic reconfigurability after fabrication, limiting their adaptability to changing tasks or environmental conditions [[claim:S03-P130]]. Promising directions include single-plane diffractive optical elements that enable compact, single-element imaging systems replacing traditional multi-lens assemblies requiring substantial physical space [[claim:S03-P114]], and complex-amplitude metasurfaces allowing monolithic integration of both phase and amplitude modulation within a single fabricated platform [[claim:S03-P117]]. These converging trends suggest that the next generation of hardware-realized diffractive neural networks will increasingly emphasize compactness, multifunctionality, and fabrication-aware design.
