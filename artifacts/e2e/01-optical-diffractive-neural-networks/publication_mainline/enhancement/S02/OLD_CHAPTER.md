Training diffractive optical neural networks requires adapting gradient-based optimization to the physical reality of diffraction, where phase profiles are learned through backpropagation through the diffraction integral. In this paradigm, the forward model governing light propagation through physically realized phase-modulating layers is itself differentiable, permitting gradient-based optimization of the physical structure directly. The training process treats the diffractive optics not merely as a fixed computational substrate but as a learnable function whose parameters—the spatially varying phase shifts imparted by each layer—are optimized through standard gradient descent procedures adapted to the continuous complex-valued field amplitudes. By embedding the physical diffraction model within the optimization loop, diffractive networks achieve all-optical inference without electronic intermediate steps during deployment. The key insight underlying this approach is that the diffraction physics, typically treated as a fixed constraint in classical optics, can instead serve as a differentiable computational graph whose internal parameters are tuned through iterative optimization.

The transition from simulation to hardware introduces computational and practical challenges that require specialized training approaches. Directly inserting discrete device mappings into differentiable optical neural network backpropagation breaks the gradient chain, necessitating physics-aware differentiable codesign to maintain trainability. Despite these challenges, end-to-end differentiable training of diffractive neural networks enables all-optical inference through unknown random diffusers, achieving blind testing accuracy approaching eighty percent for handwritten digit classification by learning phase profiles that compensate for scattering. The broadband single-pixel diffractive network demonstrated in this work surpassed the performance of monochrome configurations using significantly more output detectors, proving the improved information processing capacity of wavelength-multiplexed designs. These results highlight that successful training must account for the coupled modulation functions across wavelengths, which are tightly constrained by material dispersion properties. The fact that a single output pixel could outperform a differential configuration with twenty detectors underscores the importance of leveraging the full spectral bandwidth available in the training data.

Once trained, diffractive networks can exploit additional degrees of freedom such as polarization and wavelength to perform multifunctional computing. Polarization multiplexing in a single diffractive network enables the all-optical implementation of multiple complex-valued arbitrary linear transformations simultaneously at the same output field of view [REF:04c16f79232a1dba281da8a2626e1933bcb10641]. In practical applications, these different transformations can cover various machine vision tasks such as detection, classification, and localization of objects, programmed into different input and output polarization states. Physics-informed diffractive neural networks can approximate the inverse transmission matrix of speckle patterns to enable high-speed all-optical decryption of ciphertexts in real-time, eliminating the computational latency caused by optoelectronic conversion [REF:c54c11ac9f68c6de49d119f78ec15b47d4e1ac69]. Two-dimensional photonic crystal diffractive optical neural networks integrate on-chip parallel classification schemes capable of multi-wavelength parallel processing, overcoming the inherent limitations of traditional single-wavelength operation [REF:3ba89be3519e5be4bb45c0cbc483536e1db8bb63]. By establishing a wavelength-diffraction joint modulation model, these architectures demonstrate exceptional performance in image and video classification tasks while enabling potential applications in real-time multi-object tracking and three-dimensional reconstruction.

Physics-informed regularization strategies and fabrication-aware constraints critically shape the optimization landscape for real-world deployment. Physics-informed optimization strategies, including multi-wavelength weighted optimization and dispersion formula calculations such as the Sellmeier equation, are employed to design multilayer thin-film structures that correct aberrations like Zernike terms while managing fabrication constraints [REF:42e53c9c7630eb735f9a67f329c948423910cd8d][REF:c977f316916c59a12a847854a4eef6f49381b590]. Such approaches reduce average reflectance substantially compared to traditional anti-reflection coatings and achieve wavefront root-mean-square errors well below one milliwavelength, demonstrating the precision achievable through collaborative multi-objective optimization. However, fabrication variability introduces significant uncertainty in broadband diffractive neural network performance, particularly at shorter wavelengths where small differences in input wavelengths amplify structural errors [REF:34d7ba5c285f38e34ad058f6b2f4e7e55c2fc224]. For instance, relatively large uncertainty in the size of diffractive elements may explain low performance observed for closely spaced wavelength pairs, and single- and two-layer networks designed to classify multiple wavelengths in parallel performed poorly due to misclassification stemming from fabrication-induced deviations. These findings underscore that successful training must account not only for idealized diffraction physics but also for the imperfections inherent in physical realization.

These training paradigms collectively enable diffractive networks to address diverse applications ranging from image classification to autonomous driving tasks. Hybrid optical-electronic convolutional neural networks utilize optimized diffractive optics where color-sensitive kernels are employed to manage spectral information, often requiring narrow bandpass filters for monochromatic imaging at the cost of color data [REF:b06d7f7742ed94078f512011968ae123e576b59f]. Diffractive neural networks have been extended to perform all-optical segmentation tasks relevant to autonomous driving, indicating the scalability of training paradigms to high-dimensional visual data. Together, these developments illustrate how the convergence of gradient-based learning, physics-informed regularization, and fabrication-aware design choices positions diffractive optical networks as viable candidates for specialized high-throughput computing applications, even as challenges in bridging the simulation-to-reality gap persist.
