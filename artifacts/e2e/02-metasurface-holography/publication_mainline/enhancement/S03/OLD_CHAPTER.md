The translation of metasurface holograms from computational concept to physical device has been fundamentally reshaped by inverse design methodologies, which address the high-dimensional parameter spaces inherent in nanophotonic optimization problems [REF:2d3412a215f8b6d1d395bfaeec7ec0379c2e132e][REF:2326997ee1d85fcb31f51c4488cdfaa40639d131][REF:aed4e02073734bc74e53063c0b555f881cc3221d]. These approaches have evolved from intuitive systematic fine-tuning to data-driven machine learning frameworks—including deep neural networks, physics-informed diffusion models, and adjoint-based gradient evaluation—to streamline the computational translation of desired optical responses into nanostructure geometries [REF:2a3fb330497fc0883c9a7acc35de2b94ad7c1af0][REF:9c20a790aa75f24b6f5a130711645c978484455e][REF:identity-fallback:e49491a3a2a5b595][REF:doi:10.1002/adma.202312303][REF:doi:10.1038/s41467-024-53749-6][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118]. For metasurface holograms specifically, topology optimization and deep learning-based schemes map electromagnetic performance parameters directly to structural parameters, enabling complex wavefront manipulation beyond conventional geometric phase constraints. This paradigm shift reflects a broader recognition that inverse design constitutes a high-dimensional, nonconvex optimization problem increasingly addressed through algorithmic approaches rather than traditional heuristic methods. Computational efficiency gains have been substantial: paradigms utilizing spatial coupled mode theory or adjoint methods deliver speedups of several orders of magnitude over full-wave simulations, enabling the optimization of complex geometries such as hollow structures and non-uniform bianisotropic unit cells.

Yet this computational progress reveals a critical gap when moving from design to fabrication. Inverse design methodologies generate complex continuous or discrete geometric profiles that require binarization and discretization steps before they become physically fabricable [REF:a801ee6d75d827ef0b99528619e877e3aac72277][REF:e4817fc664074ddc772d9ae198871fc6cb7f8b7b][REF:60df2f3598ea7d56ae24a649121907a6769eb444]. The translation from computational inverse design to physical metasurface structures introduces structural disorder—including sidewall angle deviations, feature roughness, and placement errors—that systematically degrades holographic diffraction efficiency and image quality below theoretical design predictions [REF:a801ee6d75d827ef0b99528619e877e3aac72277][REF:6898fb9b38a16056d0997abe1c40d3dcfc2c0b82]. Machine-learning and optimization-based approaches allow constituent unit cells of optimized metasurfaces to adopt completely different primitives from their neighbors, a flexibility necessary to match desired surface parameters but one that introduces geometric discrepancies during fabrication. Furthermore, inverse design algorithms often generate target phase profiles or transfer matrices that are not physically realizable due to fabrication constraints, creating a persistent mismatch between idealized computational designs and feasible physical structures. The binarization process itself must be repeated multiple times for large-area metasurfaces, increasing computational complexity and compounding the risk of introducing fabrication-incompatible features.

Metasurface holograms are typically fabricated using research-scale techniques such as electron-beam lithography (EBL) and focused ion beam (FIB) writing, which offer high resolution but suffer from inherently low throughput due to serial processing limitations [REF:6898fb9b38a16056d0997abe1c40d3dcfc2c0b82][REF:534d7537d2b14d22159403688f4c5f0db3ea42ac]. These methods have enabled demonstrations of dielectric metasurfaces utilizing titanium dioxide on quartz or gallium nitride on sapphire substrates that achieve high optical efficiency in the visible spectrum, establishing viable material platforms for holographic applications [REF:a148605d4ba86a5a0712cb63c088900b39561267][REF:doi:10.1002/adma.202312303][REF:doi:10.1038/s41467-024-53749-6][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118]. However, the serial nature of EBL and FIB processes means that each meta-atom must be individually patterned, making large-area holographic metasurface production impractical. Recent work has demonstrated that parametrizing single meta-atoms in terms of relevant geometrical parameters—such as optimizing only width for rectangular meta-atoms—can reduce computational cost while still achieving functional designs. Nevertheless, even these optimizations assume perfect fabrication conditions that rarely obtain in practice.

Scalable manufacturing strategies such as nanoimprint lithography (NIL) and roll-to-roll processing have been proposed to overcome the throughput bottlenecks of serial lithography [REF:dfa21cd70a71ab37879ca600843b21680f16e478][REF:doi:10.1002/adma.202312303][REF:doi:10.1038/s41467-024-53749-6][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118]. These approaches could theoretically replicate complex meta-surface patterns across meter-scale areas in a single imprint step. Yet their ability to maintain the nanometer-scale fidelity required for high-efficiency holographic reconstruction remains largely unvalidated at production-relevant scales. A significant barrier is that TiO₂ preparation by atomic layer deposition technology requires extended processing times, while silicon metasurfaces exhibit lower diffraction efficiency, complicating the materials selection for large-area holographic devices. The quantitative relationship between fabrication-induced defect statistics and holographic image degradation has not been systematically characterized across area scales—a gap that limits confidence in scaling current laboratory demonstrations to commercial production.

Hybrid algorithms combining data-driven machine learning with heuristic or rigorous simulation methods have emerged as a new class of efficient inverse design methodology, mitigating the generalization limitations of neural networks when designing complex nanophotonic structures [REF:c9e5de816ca2356737e7f23532658ef28e6b2738][REF:0c04bf631766f465f0b30dcd07a342ff014ed8f3]. These hybrid pipelines leverage neural networks for rough global estimation followed by iterative optimization for local refinement, simultaneously overcoming the individual limitations of each technique. Deep learning-based inverse design schemes significantly reduce the design cycle and complexity for metasurfaces, although they require high-dimensional input electromagnetic performance parameters that can constrain model generalizability [REF:76a83ea3baed75ad88b7f0782eb9a694a330c853][REF:a52cc1154f412a7274d108ad735e0bec014d002f][REF:doi:10.1002/adma.202312303][REF:doi:10.1038/s41467-024-53749-6][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118]. Addressing these challenges will require co-design frameworks that integrate fabrication-aware constraints directly into the inverse design loop, ensuring that generated geometries remain within the achievable tolerance windows of scalable manufacturing processes. Until such integration is achieved, the gap between computationally optimal holographic metasurfaces and manufacturable devices will persist as a fundamental bottleneck for the field.
