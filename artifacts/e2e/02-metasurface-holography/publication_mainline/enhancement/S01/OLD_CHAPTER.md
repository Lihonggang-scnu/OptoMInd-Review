Metasurfaces enable unprecedented control over light's intrinsic properties, including phase [REF:6898fb9b38a16056d0997abe1c40d3dcfc2c0b82][REF:534d7537d2b14d22159403688f4c5f0db3ea42ac], polarization, amplitude, and orbital angular momentum, through subwavelength nanostructured elements that manipulate light-matter interactions at the nanoscale. These artificially structured two-dimensional materials achieve lightweight, easily integrable, and multidimensional optical control by encoding spatially varying patterns or material compositions that provide high degrees of freedom in designing spatial inhomogeneity over an optically thin surface [REF:54e53e2aba40ccb8b0f1fa5b22deb4dfe9614917][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. This capacity to engineer electromagnetic boundary conditions at subwavelength scales has transformed metasurfaces into a foundational platform for holographic wavefront manipulation, replacing bulky refractive optics with planar equivalents that can be fabricated using semiconductor-compatible processes.

The propagation of electromagnetic wave fields in free space is governed by the Helmholtz equation for time-harmonic fields, which serves as the fundamental physical constraint for all metasurface holographic wavefront engineering [REF:262de4d6b9107f243bba6c31015fdae5841c9982][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Within this framework, the phase profile imposed by a metasurface determines the reconstructed holographic image through interference between the desired signal and conjugate terms. The spatial distribution of individual meta-atom geometries encodes the required phase delays, with each unit cell functioning as a localized scattering element whose effective refractive index and dimensions govern the local phase response. Understanding this physical foundation is essential for appreciating how different phase control mechanisms map structural parameters onto the electromagnetic boundary conditions required for holographic reconstruction.

Geometric (Pancharatnam-Berry phase) and propagation phase are distinct mechanisms for metasurface holographic wavefront control, where geometric phase relies on the spatial rotation of anisotropic nanostructures to impart a phase shift proportional to twice the orientation angle, while propagation phase arises from the optical path length variation within resonant or dielectric nanostructures [REF:867c87ae195e0a5c2016732e0f43a8c703a7a788][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. This dichotomy defines the primary design landscape for holographic metasurfaces, with each mechanism carrying distinct implications for efficiency, bandwidth, and operational flexibility. The choice between them, or their combination, determines not only the achievable diffraction efficiency but also the spectral purity, polarization requirements, and fabrication tolerance of the resulting holographic device.

Geometric phase (Pancharatnam-Berry) control relies on the spatial variation of nanoparticle orientation to induce a polarization-dependent phase shift, where left- and right-handed circularly polarized incident light generate distinct focal points or wavefronts [REF:9f2e8ccdeff1319517981790c9a85ed8743b13db][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. When an anisotropic nanostructure is rotated by an angle theta relative to the laboratory frame, the cross-polarized component of transmitted light acquires a geometric phase of plus or minus 2theta, independent of the operating wavelength. This achromatic property makes geometric phase an attractive choice for broadband holographic applications, as the phase profile remains constant across a wide spectral range. However, the mechanism is inherently polarization-selective: only one circular polarization state couples efficiently to the desired output channel, halving the theoretical maximum diffraction efficiency at approximately 25% for unpolarized illumination. This polarization sensitivity enables powerful multiplexing capabilities--different holographic images can be encoded for orthogonal circular polarizations within the same metasurface--but simultaneously constrains the usable degrees of freedom when polarization diversity is not exploited.

Resonant metasurfaces utilize bound states in the continuum (BICs) or Fano resonances to achieve high-quality factor resonances and strong electric field confinement, enabling efficient phase modulation through dispersive spectral features [REF:cc73f5040c5d928b3ca5aeeb38e0df04450efc53][REF:42f2fcc893f3a45ac750a8ffe67752f8533c0c61][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Unlike geometric phase, propagation phase depends on the resonance condition of the meta-atom, with the accumulated phase shift determined by the optical path length traversed within the nanostructure. By tuning the geometric dimensions--such as the width, height, and shape of dielectric pillars--the resonance frequency and associated phase response can be precisely engineered across the full 0 to 2pi range. Resonant mechanisms typically offer higher efficiency than geometric phase because they do not sacrifice half the incident power to polarization conversion; however, the phase response is strongly wavelength-dependent, limiting operational bandwidth. The quality factor of the resonance dictates the trade-off between phase resolution and spectral selectivity, with higher-Q resonances providing sharper phase transitions but narrower operational bandwidths.

Hybrid metal-dielectric metasurfaces combine metallic and dielectric components to exploit both plasmonic field confinement and low-loss dielectric resonances, offering a pathway to balance efficiency and field enhancement [REF:cc73f5040c5d928b3ca5aeeb38e0df04450efc53][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Metallic components provide strong near-field localization and enhanced light-matter interaction through plasmonic resonances, while dielectric elements contribute lower ohmic losses and support Mie-type resonances with favorable radiation characteristics. This combined approach seeks to capture the field-confinement benefits of plasmonics alongside the transmission efficiency of all-dielectric platforms, though practical implementations must navigate fabrication complexity and interfacial losses at metal-dielectric boundaries. Hybrid designs have demonstrated intermediate performance between purely plasmonic and purely dielectric architectures, making them particularly relevant for applications requiring both strong field enhancement and reasonable transmission efficiency.

Chromatic dispersion inherent to resonant phase control mechanisms necessitates strategies such as multi-wavelength multiplexing or dedicated waveguide channels for different colors to maintain image quality across the visible spectrum [REF:94ab4c73d8ea40f73635551d123a18a300d824cb][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Because the phase response of resonant meta-atoms varies rapidly with wavelength near resonance, a single metasurface optimized for a specific wavelength will produce significant chromatic aberration when illuminated with broadband or multi-color light. This limitation is particularly acute for white-light holography, where maintaining image fidelity across red, green, and blue channels requires either careful dispersion engineering of individual meta-atoms or architectural solutions that separate color channels spatially or spectrally. Several approaches have emerged, including the use of aperiodic meta-atom arrangements, dispersion-compensated supercells, and multi-layer stacks that independently address different spectral bands. The fundamental challenge remains that any resonant mechanism intrinsically ties phase response to wavelength, making truly achromatic operation without polarization exploitation an open problem.

Dielectric metasurfaces utilizing high-refractive-index materials such as titanium dioxide on quartz or gallium nitride on sapphire substrates can achieve high optical efficiency in the visible spectrum, providing a physical basis for efficient phase control compared to metallic plasmonic alternatives [REF:a148605d4ba86a5a0712cb63c088900b39561267][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) metasurfaces fabricated by electron-beam lithography have demonstrated diffraction efficiencies exceeding 75% in the visible range, substantially outperforming comparable gold-based plasmonic structures limited by absorption losses. Gallium nitride (GaN) platforms similarly offer high refractive indices with low extinction coefficients, supporting both electric and magnetic dipole resonances that enable complete 2pi phase coverage with minimal loss. The choice of dielectric material thus represents a critical design parameter, balancing refractive index magnitude, transparency window, and fabrication compatibility against the target application's efficiency and spectral requirements. As fabrication techniques advance, alternative high-index materials such as silicon nitride and tantalum pentoxide are emerging as viable options for visible-range holographic applications.

The inverse design of metasurfaces for holographic wavefront control is fundamentally an ill-posed problem where determining the physical structure from a desired spectral response requires resolving ambiguities in phase and amplitude coupling [REF:9c20a790aa75f24b6f5a130711645c978484455e][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Practical inverse-design problems are often under-constrained or evolve dynamically, such as intelligent metasurfaces with shifting objectives or fabrication limits, necessitating representation learning approaches to mitigate these challenges [REF:1710ffd8f90365fdbef063f85605601e2c7fc9f1]. Inverse design methods, including deep learning and adjoint optimization, are increasingly used to navigate the complex parameter space of these phase mechanisms, though they often require significant computational resources and large training datasets [REF:930088708bb53112c4de0ace1dbad227dbc0ba5d][REF:4cad266be07154f9e36a00e0ca18bf6c548b483f][REF:76a83ea3baed75ad88b7f0782eb9a694a330c853][REF:5e3fe8b0ff11e144284f2eb96a0ba8ebc923f20d][REF:2ada810790d24fbb122c780bc6cee82d52c37420][REF:doi:10.1515/nanoph-2017-0118][REF:ca97361d48d1d83e840fb861f0849153191b91d7][REF:12ff09ff1c00b43927951becc8f09aeda28b1657]. Data-driven frameworks have become essential tools for mapping desired holographic phase profiles to optimal meta-atom geometries, accelerating the transition from analytical design rules to numerically optimized structures that push beyond the performance limits of intuitive approaches. The growing integration of machine learning into metasurface design workflows reflects the increasing sophistication of holographic targets, where traditional parametric optimization becomes computationally prohibitive as the number of design variables and performance constraints grows.
