Optical interconnect fabrics represent the most mature pathway for deploying scalable photonic computing systems in real-world architectures, addressing the fundamental bottleneck of data movement that increasingly constrains system-level performance. As transistor scaling continues to deliver incremental improvements in logic density, system-level performance and energy efficiency are increasingly constrained by data movement, memory bandwidth, and interconnect power consumption—challenges that become especially acute in large-scale AI systems where communication and I/O frequently dominate both latency and energy budgets [[claim:S05-P041]]. Optical signal processing offers wide-bandwidth, cost-effectiveness, and power efficiency advantages over electrical domain processing for telecommunications and interconnect applications, making it an attractive alternative for next-generation computing fabrics. Silicon photonics has emerged as a promising technology to relieve these bottlenecks, offering high bandwidth density, low propagation loss, and natural support for broadcast and wavelength-division multiplexing [[claim:S05-P113]]. These physical advantages position photonics as a critical enabler for breaking through the interconnect wall that limits current electronic systems.

A scaling hierarchy for optical interconnects reveals distinct technology requirements at chip-to-chip, board-to-board, and rack-to-rack tiers, with heterogeneous integration offering the highest near-term return for photonic computing deployment. Heterogeneous back-end-of-line (BEOL) integration of thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) with active silicon photonics enables the co-packaging of high-performance optical transceivers and photonic computing accelerators on a single substrate, addressing critical scaling bottlenecks in chip-to-chip interconnects [[claim:S05-P009]]. This platform combines TFLN modulators with high-speed germanium photodetectors on a single silicon chip, retaining CMOS compatibility while leveraging mature silicon photonics process design kits. Lithium niobate photonics enables dynamic optical routing and wavelength management through electric-field poling, which allows local inversion of spontaneous polarization at room temperature to engineer electro-optic coefficients for active interconnect control [[claim:S05-P025]]. On-chip dielectric metasurfaces provide a CMOS-compatible mechanism for controlling the degrees of freedom of the on-chip light field, serving as a foundational technology for dense photonic integration [[claim:S05-P033]]. Together, these integration approaches form the foundation for scalable co-packaged optical interconnect architectures.

While silicon photonics offers high bandwidth density and low propagation loss, the available evidence does not yet establish whether optical interconnects provide orders-of-magnitude improvements in bandwidth-density and energy-per-bit over electrical links for chip-to-chip and board-to-board scaling in photonic computing systems [[claim:S05-P017]]. This remains an open question requiring further experimental validation across representative photonic computing workloads. Current demonstrations show promising device-level metrics, but system-level benchmarks comparing end-to-end energy per bit and effective bandwidth between competing interconnect technologies remain limited. A clear path toward large-scale photonics-empowered systems requires system-level integration across devices, circuits, architectures, interconnect fabrics, and learning algorithms under constraints qualitatively different from electronics.

Dynamic optical routing and wavelength management are enabled by lithium niobate photonics through electric-field poling, which allows local inversion of spontaneous polarization at room temperature to engineer electro-optic coefficients for active interconnect control [[claim:S05-P025]]. However, thermal crosstalk in integrated thermo-optic attenuators and phase shifters creates undesired power fluctuations that limit the density and reliability of optical interconnect fabrics [[claim:S05-P114]]. PIC/EPIC implementations must obey curvilinear geometries, limited routing resources, strict fabrication rules, and strong sensitivity to process variation and thermal effects, all of which directly impact loss, crosstalk, tuning power, and yield. Managing these thermal interactions becomes increasingly challenging as interconnect density scales, requiring careful thermal isolation strategies and potentially adaptive compensation schemes.

Nanophotonic inverse design techniques have become essential for creating the compact, non-intuitive optical components required for dense interconnect fabrics, moving beyond conventional template-based designs [[claim:S05-P001]]. Machine learning-based inverse design techniques, including generative deep neural networks and adversarial autoencoders, enable the rapid optimization of nanophotonic components such as grating couplers and wavelength demultiplexers required for dense optical interconnect fabrics [[claim:S05-P065]]. Deep learning-based inverse design methods, including generative models and representation learning, are increasingly used to optimize nanophotonic structures for specific functionalities such as spectral tuning and phase control [[claim:S05-P073]]. Recent reviews summarize advancements in inverse design techniques applied to nanophotonics, highlighting the merger of nanotechnology and photonics enabled by optimization algorithms and deep learning [[claim:S05-P080]]. Topology optimization utilizes straightforward update rules based on fast gradient computation to rapidly converge in local optima of material structures, while heuristic methods can navigate complex energy landscapes and search for global optima of design structures.

Machine learning-driven approaches significantly accelerate the design cycle of photonic interconnect components by bypassing computationally expensive electromagnetic simulations that traditionally bottleneck optimization schemes [[claim:S05-P066]]. Discriminative machine learning models serve as universal predictors in the design space, where integrated structures hold the ability to approximate arbitrary optical responses. Latent space representations and dimensionality reduction techniques facilitate knowledge discovery in nanophotonics, allowing designers to navigate complex design spaces for optical interconnect structures more efficiently than exhaustive search methods [[claim:S05-P067]]. Machine learning-driven inverse design frameworks integrate deep learning and optimization methods to efficiently design nanophotonic devices such as metasurfaces and silicon photonic components, overcoming the limitations of traditional computational approaches in material discovery and device optimization [[claim:S05-P060]]. Physics-informed neural networks and deep convolutional mixture density networks enable the inverse design of layered photonic structures, providing a mechanism for optimizing device geometries to meet specific scattering or transmission requirements in interconnect components [[claim:S05-P049]].

Despite these advances, several challenges persist including neural network generalization limitations, fabrication constraints, and thermal crosstalk that limit interconnect density and reliability. Neural networks used in photonic design often struggle to generalize beyond their training data distribution, necessitating hybrid algorithms or physics-informed constraints to ensure robust performance in novel interconnect configurations [[claim:S05-P013]]. Inverse design in nanophotonics faces a non-uniqueness problem where networks can generate different meta-atom geometries yielding similar optical properties [[claim:S05-P085]]. Inverse design methodologies subject to strict foundry fabrication constraints, such as minimum length scale and brush size limits, enable the creation of photonic components that are manufacturable at scale, addressing a critical barrier to deploying complex optical interconnect fabrics in computing systems [[claim:S05-P105]]. The integration of optical interconnects with photonic computing accelerators requires co-optimization of device-level inverse design with system-level architectural constraints to manage thermal and loss bottlenecks, as demonstrated in recent photonic neural network design frameworks [[claim:S05-P125]]. Furthermore, an important open question remains whether current machine learning approaches for photonic inverse design are primarily focused on component-level optimization rather than system-level co-design of interconnect fabrics with computing accelerators, representing a gap in scalable distributed photonic computing research. Bridging this gap between component-level design automation and system-level architecture exploration will be critical for realizing the full potential of optical interconnect fabrics in large-scale photonic computing systems.
