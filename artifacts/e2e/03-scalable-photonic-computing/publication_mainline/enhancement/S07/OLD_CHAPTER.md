Electronic-photonic co-design represents a paradigm shift in scalable photonic computing, recognizing that the photonic substrate cannot serve as a drop-in replacement for electronic hardware but instead requires joint optimization across the algorithm-hardware stack. Treating the photonic substrate as a distinct computational layer with specific constraints—analog nature, limited precision, and slow reconfiguration—necessitates algorithmic adaptations such as quantization and sparsity rather than attempting to emulate digital electronic behavior [[claim:S07-P009]]. This perspective marks a departure from earlier approaches that attempted to force photonic devices into pre-existing electronic design paradigms. The integration of nanophotonic inverse design with artificial intelligence represents a corresponding shift from intuition-based design to data-driven co-design, where algorithmic adaptations are tightly coupled with hardware geometry optimization to achieve superior device performance [[claim:S07-P008]]. Together, these methodological shifts define the foundation upon which all subsequent co-design strategies are built.

At the device level, nanophotonic inverse design methodologies have evolved from intuition-based approaches to data-driven frameworks where deep learning models simultaneously optimize physical variables and geometrical parameters to meet functional requirements [[claim:S07-P001]]. Deep learning-based inverse design methods, including generative models and representation learning, have become standard tools for optimizing nanophotonic structures under complex constraints [[claim:S07-P073]]. These techniques overcome the limitations of intuition-based designs, enabling the implementation of non-intuitive optical devices ranging from beam splitters to metalenses and polarizationsensitive displays. Topology optimization utilizes straightforward update rules based on fast gradient computation to rapidly converge in local optima of material structures, while heuristic methods can navigate more complex energy landscapes in search of global optima. Discriminative learning models serve as universal predictors in the design space, where integrated structures hold the ability to approximate arbitrary optical responses, providing a foundation for the next layer of acceleration.

Deep learning surrogate models and discriminative machine learning architectures dramatically accelerate this co-design loop by replacing computationally expensive electromagnetic simulations with rapid forward and inverse prediction networks. Neural networks can take device structure data—shape, depth, and permittivity—to predict the optical response of nanostructures as forward networks, serving as fast approximations of optical response instead of using computationally heavy numerical methods [[claim:S07-P041]]. Conversely, these networks can also accept an optical response as input and provide approximate solutions for the nanostructure geometry, enabling true inverse design. Once trained to represent the topology-to-response relationship and vice versa, a network can design nanostructured geometry in a fraction of a second, compared to the hours or days required by iterative full-wave simulations. Discriminative machine learning models similarly serve as efficient surrogates for inverse design in nanophotonics by bypassing the computationally expensive electromagnetic simulations typically required in optimization schemes [[claim:S07-P065]]. These surrogate approaches fundamentally change the economics of the co-design optimization loop.

Reinforcement learning algorithms further extend automated inverse design into high-dimensional, non-convex parameter spaces where traditional heuristic methods risk converging to local minima [[claim:S07-P042]]. Machine learning optimizers and meta-optimization techniques serve as critical design automation tools for nanophotonic inverse design, bridging the gap between abstract algorithmic objectives and physical device constraints [[claim:S07-P059]]. By framing inverse design as a sequential decision-making problem, reinforcement learning agents can explore vast design spaces more effectively than gradient-based methods alone, discovering structural motifs that might be inaccessible through conventional optimization trajectories. This capability becomes especially important as device complexity increases and the dimensionality of the design space grows beyond what manual exploration can manage.

Physics-informed neural networks and graph neural networks address a fundamental challenge in inverse design by explicitly encoding physical device structures and partial differential equation constraints directly into machine learning models, ensuring that algorithmic outputs remain physically realizable without post-hoc correction [[claim:S07-P049]]. Physics-informed graph neural networks enable co-design by explicitly encoding physical device structures into machine learning models, allowing for the inverse design of integrated photonic components while respecting underlying physical constraints [[claim:S07-P033]]. Physics-informed neural networks and adjoint methods provide algorithmic frameworks for solving PDE-constrained inverse problems in photonic design, enabling the optimization of device structures by embedding physical laws directly into the loss function [[claim:S07-P097]]. This approach ensures that designed photonic devices adhere to fundamental physical laws, improving fabrication fidelity and reducing the need for iterative refinement after initial design generation.

Despite these advances, heuristic optimization methods frequently produce target phase profiles or transfer matrices that are not physically realizable, creating significant performance mismatches when projected onto feasible metasurface structures [[claim:S07-P075]]. When idealized designs are projected onto feasible metasurface structures, the mismatch leads to significant deviations in optical behavior and degraded diffractive optical neural network performance. Efforts to mitigate this issue through smoothness regularization have generally been inadequate, as smoother profiles limit expressivity without ensuring true physical feasibility. Physics-inspired progressive inverse optimization frameworks such as SP²RINT mitigate this mismatch between idealized designs and physical realizability by incorporating PDE constraints progressively during training, alternating between relaxed optimization and inverse metasurface projection to ensure Maxwell's equations are satisfied at every iteration [[claim:S07-P076]]. This progressive enforcement approach addresses the scalability bottleneck of simulation-in-the-loop methods while maintaining physical consistency.

The inverse problem in nanophotonics faces a persistent non-uniqueness challenge where different meta-atom geometries can yield identical or similar optical properties, requiring structured parameterization to ensure numerical stability and identifiability during the co-design optimization process [[claim:S07-P083]]. Without careful parameterization, the optimization landscape becomes ill-conditioned, with multiple geometric configurations mapping to equivalent optical responses and making it difficult to identify globally optimal designs. Structured parameterization strategies—including physiologically consistent relationships enforced by construction and physics-informed initialization—improve robustness under sparse sampling conditions and reduce convergence to implausible local minima, ensuring that the co-design process produces designs that are both performant and manufacturable.

At the system level, heterogeneous back-end-of-line integration platforms combining thin-film lithium niobate with active silicon photonics enable the monolithic co-design of photonic computing substrates and electronic control circuitry on a single integrated platform, bringing photonic and electronic functionality closer together than discrete packaging allows [[claim:S07-P017]]. This monolithic integration strategy allows the photonic computing substrate and its associated electronic control circuitry to be optimized together rather than as separately designed modules that must later be assembled, creating the conditions necessary for dense, high-performance photonic computing arrays.

Analog nanophotonic computing systems demonstrate that practical co-design requires adapting algorithms to the inherent analog nature and limited precision of photonic hardware rather than forcing digital emulation, with silicon photonic deep learning engines enabling tiled optical processing [[claim:S07-P021]]. Rather than attempting to discretize the continuous optical domain into artificial digital representations, these systems embrace the analog computation model, developing training and inference procedures that are native to the photonic substrate. This approach yields efficiency gains that would be impossible under a digital emulation paradigm, as the algorithm exploits the natural parallelism and energy efficiency of optical interference and diffraction phenomena.

Diffractive optical neural networks implement a particularly pure form of co-design where physical diffractive layers are jointly optimized with the algorithmic function, enabling compact on-chip machine learning without iterative electronic feedback loops [[claim:S07-P057]]. In this paradigm, the physical propagation medium itself constitutes part of the learned computational graph, with the diffractive layer geometries directly encoding the transformation weights. Multi-wavelength parallel computation in such networks is typically limited to a small number of widely separated wavelengths within a broad spectral band, presenting an ongoing challenge for expanding computational capacity [[claim:S07-P078]]. Integrating diffractive elements with wavelength-selective components such as photonic crystal microcavity arrays offers a pathway to overcoming this limitation while preserving the intrinsic advantages of all-optical processing.

Physical neural networks extend this philosophy further by allowing physical layers to implement controllable functions that exploit light-matter interaction directly, creating computational pathways that differ from conventional deep learning layer abstractions and offer new degrees of freedom in architectural design [[claim:S07-P081]]. This flexibility enables novel computational architectures that leverage the unique physics of optical propagation without being constrained by the architectural conventions of electronic neural networks. The resulting systems can perform computations that would require many more layers or parameters in a purely electronic implementation, achieving greater computational density per unit area.

Scalable photonic neural networks leverage surrogate scattering-matrix inverse design to map input data to reduced feature vectors, enabling efficient optical encoding and processing while mitigating the computational burden of online in-situ training on large datasets [[claim:S07-P044]]. Deep reinforcement learning combined with surrogate scattering-matrix inverse design enables automated co-optimization of photonic device geometry and system-level optical operators, allowing the hardware to be tailored for specific computational tasks such as all-optical classification [[claim:S07-P113]]. By separating the exploratory optimization phase—which can be carried out entirely in simulation—from the final deployment phase, these approaches dramatically reduce the time required to bring co-designed photonic systems from concept to functioning hardware.

Robustness to fabrication imperfections remains a critical constraint in co-designed devices, as satisfying functional specifications does not guarantee experimental performance if the device is sensitive to manufacturing variations [[claim:S07-P084]]. Inverse design methodologies incorporating strict foundry fabrication constraints, such as minimum length scale limits, enable the creation of photonic devices that are both optically optimized and manufacturable, bridging the gap between theoretical performance and practical implementation [[claim:S07-P105]]. Incorporating fabrication-aware constraints directly into the inverse design objective function ensures that the resulting designs can be reliably reproduced at scale, a prerequisite for any technology targeting commercial deployment.

Environmental vulnerabilities compound fabrication sensitivity in operational systems. Thermal crosstalk in integrated thermo-optic phase shifters imposes a fundamental boundary condition on electronic control overhead, necessitating co-designed calibration strategies or distributed tuning architectures to mitigate undesired optical interference in dense photonic circuits [[claim:S07-P116]]. Fabrication-induced phase variations introduce optical phase noise that degrades matrix-vector multiplication accuracy in coherent MZI-based nanophotonic neural networks, requiring co-design approaches that incorporate fabrication tolerance into the algorithmic mapping or calibration process [[claim:S07-P117]]. Photonic neural networks leveraging resonator nonlinearities for activation functions face additional vulnerability to temperature fluctuations and mechanical vibrations, which can adversely impact performance and require co-designed stabilization strategies [[claim:S07-P003]]. Machine learning-based image denoising concepts applied to inverse networks can improve robustness against experimental noise in these co-designed systems, demonstrating cross-domain transfer of mitigation techniques.

Machine learning-assisted tuning and inverse design frameworks ultimately serve as the critical co-design enablers that bridge the gap between high-level algorithmic objectives and low-level physical device configurations, effectively automating the mapping of abstract functions to photonic hardware layouts [[claim:S07-P122]]. Co-designed photonic computing systems leverage physics-informed constraints to address the high computational cost of traditional inverse design, thereby reducing the electronic control overhead required to map algorithmic objectives to physical hardware configurations [[claim:S07-P053]]. As these methodologies mature, they promise to transform photonic computing from a collection of specialized proof-of-concept demonstrations into a broadly accessible computational platform where algorithm developers can specify desired functionality at a high level and rely on automated co-design pipelines to produce physically realizable, fabrication-tolerant hardware implementations.
