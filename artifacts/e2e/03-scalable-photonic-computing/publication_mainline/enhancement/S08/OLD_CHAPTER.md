Scalable photonic computing stands at a methodological inflection point. Component-level demonstrations across material platforms—from silicon photonics to thin-film lithium niobate—have achieved sufficient fidelity to justify sustained investment in system-level integration [[claim:S08-P064]]. Yet the field simultaneously confronts a documented challenge: the absence of unified benchmarking protocols that account for both energy efficiency and computational fidelity across diverse photonic computing modalities, which impedes fair comparison with electronic alternatives. This synthesis evaluates the current state of the art, identifies the most consequential gaps, and assesses the realistic trajectory for deployment across optical interconnects and photonic AI accelerators.

The methodology underlying nanophotonic device design has undergone a fundamental transformation. Traditional intuition-based approaches, which relied on heuristic reasoning and incremental parameter sweeps, are being supplanted by data-driven inverse design techniques that leverage deep learning and generative AI to discover non-intuitive optical structures [[claim:S08-P001]]. Deep learning-based inverse design methods, including capsule networks and generative adversarial networks, have become standard tools for nanophotonic device optimization, though they require significant initial training resources and careful parameter tuning across logarithmic search spaces [[claim:S08-P121]]. The inverse design of nanophotonic devices is increasingly reliant on machine learning optimizers and hybrid frameworks, indicating that software-defined photonic systems are becoming a dominant paradigm for achieving compact and versatile device performance [[claim:S08-P059]].

Within this landscape, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and differentiable simulators have emerged as particularly promising frameworks. Physics-informed neural networks and deep learning architectures such as CNNs and Capsule Networks have become standard methodologies for the inverse design of nanophotonic structures, enabling optimization under complex physical constraints such as minimum feature sizes [[claim:S08-P049]]. Differentiable physical simulators and physics-informed neural operators enable scalable inverse design of photonic devices, substantially reducing the computational bottlenecks associated with traditional finite-element or finite-difference time-domain methods [[claim:S08-P089]]. These approaches incorporate governing physical equations directly into the training objective via automatic differentiation, providing hard constraints that improve the physical consistency of inverse-designed photonic components.

Progress on manufacturing and integration frontiers partially mitigates these challenges. Heterogeneous back-end-of-line (BEOL) integration platforms combining thin-film lithium niobate with active silicon photonics have been demonstrated, overcoming key obstacles in die-to-wafer bonding and multilayer integration to enable complex, high-performance photonic circuits on a single chip [[claim:S08-P014]]. Inverse design methods constrained by strict foundry fabrication limits—such as minimum length scale specifications—enable the generation of photonic device geometries that are both high-performance and manufacturable, directly addressing a critical barrier to scalable integration. On-chip dielectric metasurfaces are emerging as a key technology for scalable photonic computing given their compatibility with standard CMOS fabrication processes and their capacity to control the degrees of freedom of the on-chip light field. Surrogate-based inverse design workflows that decouple label fitting, fabrication-aware parameterization, and electromagnetic scattering further enable scalable optimization of photonic neural networks by mitigating the nonconvexity inherent in single-loop joint optimization.

The integration of artificial intelligence into nanophotonics has shifted the field from component-centric design toward system-level co-design, where AI drives both the optimization of photonic devices and the implementation of photonic-based AI accelerators [[claim:S08-P062]]. However, a one-time cost simulation is needed to train the AI, and as structures become more complex, more data is required, presenting significant barriers to scaling up this method in cutting-edge applications.

Despite these advances, AI-driven inverse design introduces substantial computational challenges. Deep learning-based models face notable overhead and remain sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, although hybrid frameworks incorporating neural embeddings are emerging to address these limitations [[claim:S08-P002]]. Purely generative approaches treat inverse design as a direct mapping problem learned through deep generative models, yet they face a persistent Constraint Rigor Gap—a difficulty in strictly enforcing hard physical or chemical constraints that are fundamental in scientific domains. These iterative-sampling paradigms require repeated evaluations, motivating exploration of generative and language-model-based alternatives that can learn direct property-to-structure mappings without repeated sampling cycles.

Critical open questions persist. It remains unclear whether component-level demonstrations showing promise translate to scalable deployment without standardized protocols for energy-per-operation and latency under realistic workload conditions [[claim:S08-P104]]. Resolving these questions through system-algorithm co-exploration and cross-platform benchmarking initiatives will be essential to determining whether photonic computing can transition from isolated demonstrators to mainstream deployment.
