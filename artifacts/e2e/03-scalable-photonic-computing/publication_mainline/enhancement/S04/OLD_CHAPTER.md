Machine learning algorithms deployed across autonomous driving, real-time speech translation, and network anomaly detection demand ever-greater computational throughput while operating under strict energy budgets. These operational demands motivate the exploration of photonic computing as an alternative to conventional electronic accelerators, particularly for workloads where latency and power consumption represent primary bottlenecks. Photonic neural networks are prioritized over purely analog electronic alternatives for specific imaging and computing tasks due to their unique capabilities in high-speed, low-latency light-based computation.

Photonic neural networks exploit the superposition of complex optical waves and multi-cascade interferometry to perform linear transformations that are fundamentally compatible with deep neural network operations [[claim:S04-P073]]. Silicon photonic deep learning engines further advance this approach by employing tiled optical matrix multiplication architectures with dynamic precision capabilities, moving toward practical analog nanophotonic computing performance [[claim:S04-P002]]. Integrated photonic tensor cores demonstrate high throughput-per-watt efficiency for inference workloads involving fixed or slowly varying linear transformations, such as parallel convolutional processing in deep neural networks. The nonlinear behavior of optical components also plays a role: photonic neural networks leverage the inherent nonlinear characteristics of optical resonators to implement activation functions, enabling all-optical inference without electronic conversion bottlenecks [[claim:S04-P001]]. Such systems encode input features onto coherent optical fields for linear transformation, achieving ultra-compact footprints suitable for deployment at the edge.

While photonic computing accelerators demonstrate superior throughput-per-watt for inference workloads that map to fixed or slowly varying linear transformations, training workloads requiring frequent weight updates and gradient computation expose fundamental mismatches between photonic hardware capabilities and algorithmic requirements. Training large-scale optoelectronic neural networks requires dual-neuron optical-artificial learning strategies, indicating that pure photonic training is currently insufficient and hybrid electronic-photonic control loops are necessary for weight updates. Online in-situ training on photonic hardware faces significant latency challenges due to high-dimensional datasets such as MNIST, limiting the feasibility of frequent weight updates required for standard backpropagation-based training loops. Algorithm-hardware co-design is critical for extending the feasible workload spectrum of scalable photonic computing, particularly by addressing the oversimplification of transfer matrices in heuristic methods which leads to unimplementable responses and performance degradation [[claim:S04-P010]]. These constraints suggest that near-term photonic AI accelerators will predominantly serve inference roles, with training performed electronically and weights periodically loaded onto the photonic engine.

Despite these training limitations, several photonic AI accelerator demonstrations have validated feasibility for inference tasks. Inverse-designed nanophotonic neural network accelerators have been demonstrated for image classification tasks using benchmark datasets such as MNIST, showing viability for ultra-compact optical computing applications. All-optical photonic neural networks can perform direct classification tasks by mapping encoded feature vectors to class channels without electronic intermediate processing, as demonstrated on the MedMNIST dataset. Photonic neural networks utilizing passive linear operators and surrogate scattering-matrix inverse design can encode reduced-dimensionality feature vectors for inference tasks, enabling scalable photonic AI acceleration without active weight updates during operation. These demonstrations collectively establish that photonic inference accelerators can achieve practical accuracy on standardized computer vision benchmarks when the computational graph is statically defined.

Algorithm-hardware co-design is critical for extending the feasible workload spectrum of scalable photonic computing. Deep learning-based inverse design methods, including differentiable surrogates and POD-DeepONet frameworks, allow for the optimization of photonic structures to better match specific AI workload requirements, representing a key co-design strategy to bridge hardware-algorithm gaps. Machine learning-based inverse design strategies, including dimensionality reduction and adversarial autoencoders, enable the rapid discovery of nanophotonic structures for specific spectral responses, serving as a critical algorithm-hardware co-design step that bridges the gap between abstract photonic device requirements and physical realization. Discriminative deep learning models significantly accelerate the optimization of nanophotonic devices by bypassing computationally expensive electromagnetic simulations, thereby enabling large-scale experimental machine learning workflows for wafer-scale photonics inverse design. Generative deep neural networks and surrogate modeling approaches expedite the complex inverse design of tunable nanophotonic devices by exploring relevant statistical structures and reserving full-wave evaluations for promising candidates only [[claim:S04-P067]]. Physics-informed neural networks and mixture density networks enable robust inverse design of photonic structures by incorporating physical constraints and handling fabrication uncertainty distributions. Deep neural network-based inverse design enables the automated creation of integrated nanophotonic devices, including power splitters with high correlation coefficients exceeding 0.995 across transmission ratios, demonstrating the feasibility of algorithm-hardware co-design for photonic AI components [[claim:S04-P077]]. Together, these co-design methodologies form a rapidly maturing ecosystem that allows photonic accelerator designers to optimize device architectures directly for target AI workloads rather than relying on generic photonic building blocks.

Several open questions remain at the intersection of photonic AI acceleration and scalable system integration. The available material does not establish whether the integration of electronic control and memory subsystems with photonic computing engines constitutes a critical architectural requirement for building functional AI accelerators, nor does it provide comprehensive benchmark data comparing these hybrid systems against pure electronic GPUs or TPUs for standard ML workloads. Similarly, it remains unclear whether current photonic AI accelerator research predominantly emphasizes component-level performance metrics—such as modulator speed and waveguide loss—rather than end-to-end system-level benchmarks against established electronic standards like FLOPS/Watt for specific AI model architectures. Finally, the extent to which algorithm-hardware co-design strategies, including quantization-aware training and sparsity exploitation, can fully compensate for the intrinsic precision limitations of photonic computing hardware when executing deep learning workloads remains an unresolved challenge. Addressing these questions will be essential for determining the practical deployment timeline and competitive positioning of photonic AI accelerators within the broader landscape of specialized computing hardware.
