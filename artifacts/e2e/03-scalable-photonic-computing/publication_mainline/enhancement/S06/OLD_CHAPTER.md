Resonant photonic components used in computing circuits are vulnerable to environmental temperature fluctuations, which induce refractive index changes that destabilize device operation and introduce thermal crosstalk errors [REF:77474cdcf483b25f73dacf20deb295cd1c2945b7]. This vulnerability is particularly acute in programmable photonic processors where resonators serve as wavelength-selective elements for routing and filtering operations. Unlike interferometric architectures that rely on precise phase matching between two arms, resonant devices depend on constructive interference within a closed loop, making them inherently sensitive to any perturbation that shifts the effective optical path length. Even small temperature variations of a fraction of a degree can detune a resonator from its designed wavelength, causing signal loss or crosstalk between adjacent channels. In dense integrated photonic systems where hundreds of resonators operate simultaneously on a single chip, maintaining thermal stability becomes a non-trivial engineering challenge that scales poorly with device count.

The inverse design process itself becomes a critical scalability constraint as the degrees of freedom in nanophotonic optimization grow combinatorially with circuit size and structural complexity. The degrees of freedom in nanophotonic inverse design problems are governed by both physical variables from Maxwell's equations and geometrical parameters derived from a choice of parameterization, creating a complex optimization landscape that hinders scalability [REF:1710ffd8f90365fdbef063f85605601e2c7fc9f1]. When both physical and geometrical degrees of freedom are large and strongly coupled, the curse of dimensionality becomes significant, requiring PDE-based constraints to provide useful inductive bias for physically consistent learning. Existing image-based photonics design studies are limited to specific structure classes, indicating a gap in global inverse design capabilities across multiple photonic structure classes using generative deep learning [REF:1788e07c1c3a24bfbd13db23c61fadb8a31227ba]. Prior knowledge of which category of structures or devices may deliver a specific functionality is needed before initiating the optimization procedure, yet human intuition on the optimal nanostructure category can often go awry when faced with competing design goals. A unified global inverse design approach that can define both materials and structure across multiple classes of photonic structures has yet to be demonstrated but could fundamentally change how designers approach photonic circuit synthesis.

Deep learning-based inverse design methods accelerate the optimization of nanophotonic device geometries, potentially reducing the engineering time required to scale photonic computing circuits compared to manual or heuristic design approaches [REF:aed4e02073734bc74e53063c0b555f881cc3221d][REF:bb82053d4229357925b28ffe647377f56d93b06e][REF:60df2f3598ea7d56ae24a649121907a6769eb444][REF:ae237e70ca4fd23980ced32406ed67668edf25c8]. Once trained, AI can generate optimized designs quickly, making them ideal for real-time applications. Moreover, it can be applied to a range of related design problems, offering flexibility that conventional optimization algorithms lack. However, a one-time cost simulation is needed to train the AI, and as structures become more complex, more data is required. Although increasing the quantity of labeled data leads to improved network performance, a major drawback of this design approach is its significant computational expense, especially when applied to advanced AI models that require enormous datasets. Recent studies attempt to reduce this one-time cost by leveraging advanced techniques such as generative AI or transfer learning, though these research directions remain nascent.

Despite numerous advancements, it is well-known that neural networks cannot generalize too far beyond the information available in the training dataset. Due to these limitations, hybrid algorithms combining deep learning and conventional optimization methods have emerged as a new class of efficient inverse design methodology [REF:0c04bf631766f465f0b30dcd07a342ff014ed8f3][REF:ae237e70ca4fd23980ced32406ed67668edf25c8]. The neural network can perform a rough estimate of the desired solution as a global search, while the iterative optimization algorithm carries out an additional refinement step as a local search. Since conventional optimization algorithms need an ideal initial condition in order to obtain the optimal result, and the neural network is restricted by its training data, the combination of both techniques can simultaneously overcome their individual limitations. Recent hybrid ML-optimization approaches have employed more advanced neural networks including generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders together with the adjoint variables method for photonic design.

Inverse design methodologies using deep learning and representation learning are critical for managing the complexity of nanophotonic structures, addressing challenges where practical problems are under-constrained or dynamically evolving [REF:22583693b2e475539a86ea1680d5f148d67f6467][REF:1710ffd8f90365fdbef063f85605601e2c7fc9f1][REF:aed4e02073734bc74e53063c0b555f881cc3221d][REF:bb82053d4229357925b28ffe647377f56d93b06e]. Advanced optimization techniques, especially gradient-based topology optimization that can handle up to one billion design variables efficiently, allow for the design of nanophotonic devices with tremendous complexity. Increasing the number of design variables will empower the invention of more sophisticated and more integrated metadevices. The current application of deep learning in nanophotonic inverse design is still limited to finding appropriate design parameters for desired spectra, using low-dimensional inputs such as spectra and diffraction efficiencies in the network. Higher dimensional data such as field intensity profiles as well as vectorial field maps carry much more information that can be used for designing functional metalenses and holograms.

Feedforward realization speed is a critical bottleneck for deep photonic neural networks in real-time AI applications, constraining the practical utility of high-complexity architectures [REF:b765388f3cc18d6e5a0507522822def0e3503ec6]. In the era of AI, the speed of feedforward realization for deep learning is of significance for real-time applications. Photonic neural networks have great superiority of processing at the speed of light, low power consumption, and low latency. Compatible learning from backpropagation directly adopts the complex-value entity to update the weight, and the compatible condition ensures that some nonlinear activations in real space can be extended directly to complex space. Deep neural networks in real space and complex space can be effectively trained as a unity, with compatible learning deserving promotion as an available protocol on deep neural network related to coherent wave physics.

Photonic crystal diffractive optical neural networks enable on-chip parallel classification using multi-wavelength inputs, offering a potential pathway to reduce electronic control overhead by leveraging spatial and spectral multiplexing for intrinsic parallelism [REF:3ba89be3519e5be4bb45c0cbc483536e1db8bb63]. By establishing a wavelength-diffraction joint modulation model, the PhC-DONN overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional on-chip diffractive networks operating in single-wavelength mode, demonstrating exceptional performance in image and video classification tasks. The PhC-DONN can be expanded to address complex scenarios such as 100-class classification tasks and high-resolution image classification, and even potential applications in real-time multi-object tracking for autonomous driving utilizing wavelength-encoded spatial coordinates. Joint optimization of device geometry and wavelength routing can further amplify throughput gains, and integration with photonic devices such as micro-ring resonators, Mach-Zehnder interferometers, and photonic crystal modulators can construct terabits per second optical computing chips.
